Switch emulation on the Mac is good now. You can finally experience great games the way they were meant to be played. But, the Switch is still the thing you take on the train, and the two don't talk to each other: saves live in different places and nothing carries over. Which means that you have to make a choice. Start a game on your laptop, and lose out on the convenience of portability? Or play it on the go, at a crummy 540p 20fps. I personally think that really stinks.

**WarpPoint finally fixes this.** Install it on both sides once. After that, any time you close a game on the Switch, its save is on the Mac a few seconds later. And of course, it goes back the other way. On the Mac that means Astris (a Ryujinx-family emulator for Apple Silicon), or a native Mac port of the game built with nxnative.

<Note label="the goal">No configs, pop-ups, or ip addresses to manage, just a clear UI on the rare chance it needs you. No more annoying SD card swaps, which means one less adapter for you to carry around.</Note>

## How it works

There are two halves:

- **On the Switch,** a small background module (a *sysmodule*, which runs alongside games on homebrewed consoles) announces itself on your Wi-Fi every couple of seconds and reads and writes saves through the console's own save service.
- **On the Mac,** a menu bar app I wrote in Swift sits on a C++ sync engine. It hears the Switch, and syncs whenever it shows up or a game closes there.

Saves are named, not located: both sides call a save `game/<title id>/<user id>`, and each side looks up where that lives. Deciding which way to copy is one small function. Each side fingerprints the save (a SHA-256 over its files) and compares that to the last version both sides agreed on. If one side changed, copy it. If both changed, ask.

## Not destroying saves

Sync is the dangerous part, as one mishap bricks a save file, so the core has to stay small and boring:

- **Backups first.** Nothing gets overwritten until it's backed up, and the last four of each save can be restored. You can also write a note on a backup ("before the final boss") and pin it to keep it forever.
- **No half-saves.** Writes land in a scratch copy and become the real save in one step, so a dropped connection leaves the old save intact. I learned this by studying how emulators manage save files.
- **Hands off running games.** A save is never touched while its game is open, on either side.
- **Only your Mac.** As of 1.12 the Switch only talks to Macs you've paired. The two do an X25519 key exchange and you check that both screens show the same 6-digit code. After that, every connection has to answer a challenge with the shared key. An unpaired Mac on the same Wi-Fi gets nothing.

On the Switch, an overlay you can open in any game shows when it last synced, with a sync-now button and a pause switch. It's also where you update the Switch side now: choose Update, the Mac sends the new files, and the Switch checks each one against its SHA-256 and swaps them in. Nothing gets installed unless you say yes on the console itself.

## Switch 2 saves, on a Switch 1

nxnative turns a Switch game you own into its own Mac app, and since 1.2 WarpPoint finds those apps and syncs them like any other game. Fire Emblem: Three Houses as a native port syncs both ways with the Switch, and I checked the same save in-game on both sides.

Xenoblade Chronicles: Definitive Edition was the fun one. The Mac port is the Switch 2 Edition, and the Switch 2 Edition upgrades the save the first time it loads it. The Switch 1 version of the game then crashes on that save. So up until 1.8, those saves had to stay on the Mac.

I diffed one save from before and after the upgrade. It goes from version 7 to version 10 and gains two things: a 960-byte block in the middle and 784 bytes at the end. Both stay empty until you start Switch 2-only content. So WarpPoint converts it back on the way to the console: set the version back, remove the two empty sections, keep everything else. It loads and plays normally on a Switch 1, with the party, items and menus intact. If a save does have Switch 2-only progress, it stays on the Mac and the library says why.

## One link, a lot more than saves

Once there's a quiet, always-on link between the Switch and the Mac, it can carry other things. Everything besides saves is a plugin, and there's a Plugins window on the Mac to turn each one on or off:

<Figure src="/projects/warppoint/plugins.jpg" alt="WarpPoint's plugins in ten columns: Lens, Album, Presence, Tap, Mods, Split, Lexicon, Chronicle, WarpDrop and Labs, each with an icon and a one-line description" wide />

- **Lens** brings Switch crash reports to the Mac and says in plain words what happened.
- **Album** drops your Switch screenshots and videos into a folder on the Mac.
- **Presence** puts what you're playing in Discord. In 1.12 it's live: Fire Emblem shows "Ch. 8 · Black Eagles" and "In battle · Turn 1", read straight off the Switch.
- **Tap** reads live, read-only numbers out of the running game (gold, renown, the unit under your cursor), and **Split** uses them to auto-split LiveSplit.
- **Lexicon** shows the game's dialogue in a Mac window, for Look Up and Anki.
- **Mods** is one mod library that goes to the Switch, Astris and nxnative apps alike.
- **WarpDrop** (1.12): drag files onto the UFO and they land in the right place on the SD card. Transfers resume, and only a paired Mac can send.

The Library also shows what's in each save without starting the game: the chapter and house in Fire Emblem, your location and party in Xenoblade (with the save's own thumbnail), hearts and rupees in Tears of the Kingdom.

Finding where a game keeps things like its dialogue is the hard part. It turns out a native Mac port is a really good place to look: I searched the Mac port's memory for Fire Emblem's dialogue pointer, then confirmed the same chain on the Switch. WarpPoint's Labs tool, Divergence, takes that further and compares the Switch with the Mac port field by field.

## Shaders

Emulators stutter the first time they see a shader, because they have to translate it right then. But the Switch already ran every shader you've seen. So the idea is: record the shaders a game uses on your Switch as you play, and the emulator could compile them ahead of time and never stutter anywhere you've already been.

The first step was checking that it's even possible. Of the 223 shaders in Astris's cache for Fire Emblem, 221 were there byte-for-byte in the Switch's memory.

So I wrote a SaltyNX plugin (SaltyNX lets you load code into a running game) that records them. At first it hung Fire Emblem on the boot logo. I narrowed it down by building one version per step and running each on hardware, then reading SaltyNX's source, and found that SaltyNX was patching the same import twice. With that worked around, it now captures with about 0.5 µs of overhead per draw. It's still a spike, and turning the captures into Astris cache entries is the next step.

## Tested on a real Switch

Everything here was tested on an actual Switch 1, driven over the network: sys-botbase to press buttons and take screenshots, FTP for the SD card, and nxnative's control file to drive the Mac port. That's how a change gets checked in-game on both sides, not just in unit tests.

## Light enough to forget about

It's a background app, so it shouldn't cost you anything while it waits. Which is especially important when we're squeezing every last inch out of the switch on a maximum overclock. It only does semi-real work during a sync.

| | Mac app | Switch module |
|---|---|---|
| Memory | about 19 MB | a little over 2 MB |
| CPU while idle | ~0.1% of one core | wakes once a second |
| Download | 1.4 MB | 0.5 MB, plus a 0.3 MB overlay |

## What's next

1.12 is still Mac only, with Ryujinx-family emulators (like Astris) and nxnative ports. The engine treats each emulator and console as a plug-in, so more emulators, Windows and Linux are still on the list. Closer up: turning those shader captures into a pre-warmed cache for Astris, Tap profiles for more games, and sending files from the Switch back to the Mac with WarpDrop.

Self-driving models get tested in simulation: you set up a scenario, run it, and see whether the car does something dangerous. Finding the scenarios that actually break a model takes thousands of runs, and a person has to babysit them: launching runs, restarting crashed simulators, deciding what to try next.

My research hands that job to an LLM agent, with one catch: **the agent proposes, and plain code decides.** Every action the agent wants to take is checked by deterministic rules before it runs. The agent gets the flexibility; the rules keep the data trustworthy.

I led this project in Dr. Yunli Shao's lab at UGA, using NVIDIA's AlpaSim simulator, and I'm first author on the paper.

## Results

- **11,000+ simulations, unattended.** 99.0% of runs produced usable data, and all 294 deliberately broken runs we planted were caught and kept out.
- **Faster search.** The agent hit a lane-keeping failure goal in a median of 19 runs, vs. 30 for Bayesian optimization.
- **A real finding about the model.** Most of its repeatable failures traced back to a steady drift to the left. Mirroring its camera image and plan flipped the drift to the right in 18 of 20 pairs.

## The agent cheated

The most interesting result was a failure. The agent learned that the easiest way to "find" a crash was to stage one that no driver could have avoided, before the model under test had even taken control. That technically counts as a failure, and it's useless. I spotted it in the logged motion and tightened what counts as a real failure.
